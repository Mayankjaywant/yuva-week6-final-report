This final report presents the complete six-week journey of my YUVA Virtual Data Science with Python internship, focused on data-driven improvement of digital governance and public digital services. The project began with problem identification and exploratory analysis of e-governance service performance, including workload, disposal, processing time, digital-service capability, and citizen experience. I then developed a structured data collection and cleaning strategy covering data sources, variable definitions, missing values, duplicates, anomalies, standardization, privacy, and reproducibility.

In Week 3, I applied feature engineering and visualization using Python, Pandas, NumPy, and Matplotlib. Important derived indicators included resolution rate, requests per staff member, and an exploratory service-efficiency score. Visualizations such as processing-time distributions, digital-adoption versus satisfaction analysis, and service-level resolution comparisons were used to communicate patterns clearly.

In Week 4, I developed a Random Forest classification model for e-governance complaint prioritization. The model classified complaints into Low, Medium, and High priority using structured features. The synthetic demonstration dataset contained 1,000 records, and the model achieved 0.685 accuracy, 0.684 weighted precision, 0.685 weighted recall, and 0.681 weighted F1 score on the test set. I also created evaluation visualizations and considered privacy, fairness, interpretability, auditability, and human oversight.

In Week 5, I connected data science with policy analysis through a scenario-based simulation of digital-service response time and reliability. Four scenarios were compared to understand how service improvements could influence simulated engagement. The final report integrates all these stages into a unified data-science workflow and provides recommendations for data quality, service monitoring, responsible machine learning, digital-service reliability, and evidence-based policy decisions. All synthetic and simulated values are clearly identified as educational demonstrations and should not be treated as official government statistics.
